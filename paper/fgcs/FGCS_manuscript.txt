 6   Abstract
 7   Industrial IoT (IIoT) networks need per-request verification, but a blockchain on the authentication path
 8   adds seconds of latency and saturates ledger capacity. We propose BC-ZTA, a three-tier blockchain-
 9   assisted zero-trust architecture in which edge policy enforcement points decide from cached, Merkle-
10   verified ledger state, while identity, trust and audit updates are written asynchronously to a permissioned
11   Hyperledger Besu (IBFT 2.0) network. We implemented the enforcement point and six baselines,
12   including real Keycloak and OpenLDAP services and a central directory with pushed revocations, and
13   measured them on a four-validator network of seven cloud VMs. At 50 devices BC-ZTA answers in 5.4
14   ms, against 49.1 ms for a centralised directory with a 40 ms network delay and 1382 ms when every
15   request is an on-chain transaction; that synchronous design answers only 40% of requests at 200
16   devices, whereas BC-ZTA served 200 devices without failures. Event-driven invalidation enforces a
17   revocation in 1.3 s, and all 1750 attack requests were rejected. Batch anchoring cuts audit gas per record
18   68- to 1351-fold. Keycloak and OpenLDAP revoke in about 84 ms but decide 8× slower at 100 devices.
19   A central directory with pushed revocations and no blockchain matches the latency of BC-ZTA and
20   revokes in 109 ms, so the ledger buys trust distribution and verifiable audit, not speed. Both designs fail
21   closed after twice the cache lifetime when their state source is lost, and a consensus halt delays
22   revocations by minutes. Limitations are a single cloud zone, emulated devices and single-node IAM
23   products.
24   Keywords: Zero trust; Blockchain; Industrial Internet of Things; Access control; Hyperledger Besu;
25   Smart contracts; Scalability


26   1. Introduction
27   1.1. Background and Problem
28   Industrial IoT (IIoT) plants link PLCs, sensors, actuators, edge gateways and cloud analytics. Their
29   operational-technology networks used to depend on a hardened perimeter and on trust between devices
30   on the same segment. Remote maintenance and the merging of IT and OT have removed that perimeter,
31   and one compromised vendor laptop or sensor can now be the starting point of lateral movement [1, 2].
32   Zero trust, as set out in NIST SP 800-207 [3], replaces location-based trust with verification of every
33   request, and recent reviews show it spreading across application domains [4–6].
34   IIoT makes this hard for four reasons. Devices are constrained and cannot run heavy cryptography or
35   monitoring agents. Protocols and vendors differ, which complicates uniform identity management [7,
36   8]. Control loops leave only milliseconds for a security decision. And thousands of devices join, rotate
37   keys and are revoked all the time. A central identity or policy server is then a single point of failure and
38   an attractive target, and logs kept by the same operator cannot be trusted independently [5]. Following
39   NIST and the critiques of the model [9], we need per-device cryptographic identity (R1), authorisation
40   of every request by a policy decision point (PDP) enforced at a policy enforcement point (PEP) (R2),


                                                          1
41   least-privilege short-lived sessions (R3), continuous trust assessment [10] (R4), micro-segmentation
42   (R5) and tamper-evident audit (R6), with R1–R5 fitting inside control-loop latency budgets.

43   1.2. Why a Ledger, and What Is Missing
44   A permissioned blockchain can hold identity, policy and reputation state and an audit trail that plant,
45   vendor and integrator domains share without trusting any single one of them. It has been applied to zero-
46   trust IoT access [11, 12], cross-domain digital-twin access [13], IIoT data protection [14] and contract-
47   based access control [15, 16]. Three gaps remain. Many designs put the ledger on the request path, and
48   the cost shows: one Ethereum-based zero-trust framework reports latency rising from 49.33 ms to 74.0
49   ms and throughput dropping from 50 to 30.77 req/s [17]. Scaling of authentication and authorisation
50   workloads is rarely evaluated, apart from sharding-based work [18], because most studies report
51   accuracy or encryption cost [19, 20]. And continuous tamper-proof audit, named repeatedly as an open
52   problem [5], becomes expensive when every event is written on-chain.

53   1.3. Contributions
54   We design a blockchain-assisted zero-trust architecture (BC-ZTA) whose decisions do not wait for
55   consensus, bound how stale its cached state can become, and make audit cost grow more slowly than the
56   request rate. Specifically:
57   • Architecture. A three-tier design in which the PEP/PDP decide from edge-cached, Merkle-verified
58     ledger state, so the blockchain stays off the request path (Section 3).
59   • Smart contracts. Four Solidity contracts (identity registry with epoch commitments, quorum-based
60     trust reputation, ABAC access control, audit log with Merkle batch anchoring) with 15 passing unit
61     tests.
62   • Prototype and measurements. A running PEP/PDP service and load generator, six baselines (central
63     directory, local ABAC, synchronous blockchain, real Keycloak and OpenLDAP with and without a
64     PEP cache, and a central directory with pushed revocations), evaluated on seven Google Cloud VMs
65     with a four-validator Hyperledger Besu IBFT 2.0 network: latency and goodput up to 200 devices,
66     revocation delay, security tests, validator failure, central-service outage, ledger flooding, loss of the
67     state source, a consensus halt and PEP scale-out, and gas (Sections 5–6).
68   • Security analysis and its limits. An argument for each security goal under explicit assumptions,
69     together with the residual risks we found: replay across gateways, trust in a single ledger node and
70     incomplete audit (Section 4.6). A measured comparison shows where the ledger helps and where it
71     does not (Section 6.8).
72   • Bounded staleness. Event-driven invalidation reduces the mean revocation delay from 6.6 s to 1.3 s
73     (Section 6.6).
74   Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 presents the architecture, Section 4 the use case and threat
75   model, Section 5 the methodology, Section 6 the results and Section 7 concludes.

76   2. Related Work
77   2.1. Blockchain-Based Security for IIoT and IoT
78   Ledger-based security work for IIoT and IoT falls roughly into data protection, access control and
79   identity. On the data side, Essaid and Ju combine blockchain, edge computing and smart contracts for
80   integrity and threat detection [14]. For access control, Hyper-IIoT offers a contract-inspired scheme for
81   constrained devices [15], Guo applies model checking to IoT access-control contracts [16], and Zhai et


                                                         2
 82   al. authenticate cross-domain IIoT devices with Chebyshev chaotic maps [21]. Identity schemes include
 83   epoch-based batch registration with sparse Merkle trees, tested up to 7,000 devices on 20 fog nodes with
 84   under 5 MB of on-chain storage [8], and DIDs on IOTA, where generating an identifier loaded a
 85   Raspberry Pi far more than a PC [22]. For scale, DAG ledgers have been proposed for intelligent
 86   buildings [23], and a smart-city framework with a lightweight adaptive proof-of-stake reports a
 87   threefold throughput gain and 30% lower latency than conventional blockchains [24].

 88   2.2. Zero Trust for IIoT
 89   Zero-trust designs for industrial networks mostly come without a ledger. Federici et al. built a zero-trust
 90   remote-access architecture for IIoT [1], and Zanasi et al. combine micro-segmentation, SDN and a
 91   central policy layer [2]. Ma and Chiu add a risk-based access-control engine that rates a device by the
 92   behaviour of its neighbours [10], and Mthethwa et al. address non-human identities in IoT [7]. Broader
 93   overviews cover zero-trust foundation models for IoT [25] and the applications and open problems of
 94   zero trust [26].

 95   2.3. Blockchain-Assisted Zero Trust
 96   Combining the two is more recent. Aleisa pairs a blockchain-based ZTA with post-quantum and zero-
 97   knowledge techniques and reports 98% privacy preservation [11]. Nie et al. use smart contracts with
 98   inner-product encryption for 6G IoT access control and report 14% better encryption efficiency and
 99   18% lower access latency [12]. Sharma et al. add federated transfer learning for Industry 5.0 [19], and
100   Mishra et al. combine a zero-trust digital twin, intrusion detection and lightweight blockchain auditing
101   [20]. Salim et al. reach 2,500 TPS at 700 nodes with sharding driven by reinforcement learning [18].
102   Other variants target FinTech [17], clinical trials [27] and FPGA supply chains [28].

103   2.4. Alternatives That Need No Blockchain
104   Much of what a ledger does here can be done without one, so any evaluation should ask how much. On
105   the web, revocation is handled by CRLs [29] and OCSP [30], and a server can staple a signed status
106   answer to the TLS handshake [31]; a central authority that pushes signed revocations to edge
107   enforcement points is therefore an obvious rival to ledger-driven invalidation. Transparency logs such
108   as Certificate Transparency [32] give audit that third parties can check through log operators and
109   monitors, with no consensus protocol. Decentralised identifiers [33] separate control of an identifier
110   from any registry operator. Blockchain-based access control for IoT puts the policy decision or the
111   policy store on the ledger, from FairAccess [34] to scalable access-management architectures [35].
112   Among permissioned ledgers, Hyperledger Fabric uses execute-order-validate [36], whereas Istanbul
113   BFT [37], which we use, brings classical Byzantine agreement [38] to an EVM chain. Finally, an
114   Internet-wide study found many OPC UA deployments with missing or weak authentication [39], which
115   is why we insist on per-device cryptographic identity (R1). We therefore compare against central
116   directories, real Keycloak and OpenLDAP services and a central directory with pushed revocation.

126    3. Proposed Architecture
127    3.1. Design Objectives
128    D1 decisions from local state in milliseconds; D2 no single party that can change identity records, policy
129    or logs without detection (a plant authority still enrols devices, Section 4.2); D3 bounded staleness of
130    cached state; D4 write load that grows slower than the request rate; D5 a device-side workload of one
131    ECDSA signature per request and one stored Merkle path (device-side cost was not measured, Section
132    6.12).

133    3.2. Architecture Overview
134    Fig. 1 shows three tiers. Devices form Layer I (IIoT and edge). Layer II is the zero-trust security layer at
135    the edge gateway, hosting the PEP, PDP and trust engine. Layer III is the permissioned blockchain,
136    whose identity, trust, access-control and audit services are implemented as smart contracts. Gateways
137    cache Layer III state, so Layer II never waits for consensus.

140   3.3. Edge and Zero-Trust Layers
141   Sensors, actuators and PLCs hold an ECDSA key pair bound to a DID and sign every request with a
142   timestamp and a nonce. Edge gateways terminate sessions, keep a local cache of identity, trust and
143   policy state, and forward anomaly telemetry to the trust engine. On each gateway the PEP intercepts
144   every request. The PDP verifies the signature, checks the DID against a cached, Merkle-verified registry
145   entry, fetches the trust score and policy, and returns allow or deny. Micro-segmentation permits only
146   flows that hold a valid, unexpired session [2], and the trust engine turns behavioural features (rate,
147   protocol conformance, firmware attestation) into score proposals.




                                                              5
148   3.4. Blockchain Layer
149   A permissioned Hyperledger Besu network with IBFT 2.0 consensus is operated by plant, integrator and
150   vendor validators; with n validators it tolerates f = ⌊(n−1)/3⌋ Byzantine nodes. Gateways are read-only
151   clients that subscribe to events. Only authorised registrars, validators and the policy administrator send
152   transactions.

153   3.5. Identity, Trust and Access Control
154   Each device is a DID with a public-key hash and a device class. A plant-authorised registrar submits
155   registrations, which can be grouped into epochs: the registrar commits the Merkle root of a batch, and a
156   device later proves membership with a logarithmic-size path, an approach inspired by epoch-based IIoT
157   identity schemes [8]. Key rotation and revocation are single transactions that emit events. Trust scores
158   lie in [0,100] and start at 70. A score changes only when a quorum of validators proposes the same value,
159   updates are rate-limited, and an anomaly flag subtracts 20 points at once (floored at 0) until the device is
160   re-evaluated. Policies are attribute-based: for each resource the administrator sets the allowed device-
161   class range, a minimum trust score and a session TTL. A request is granted if the DID is active, the class
162   is in range and the score meets the threshold, and the grant yields a session identifier that expires after
163   the TTL or on explicit revocation.

164   3.6. Smart Contracts
165   IdentityRegistry handles registration, key update, revocation, epoch commitment and membership
166   checks. TrustReputation handles quorum voting and the anomaly flag. AccessControl holds policies,
167   evaluates requests and grants or revokes sessions. AuditLog stores per-event logs and Merkle batch
168   anchors and verifies inclusion. Source code and tests are in the public repository.

169   3.7. Continuous Verification
170   Fig. 2 summarises the workflow. Verification does not end at login: every request is re-evaluated against
171   current cached trust, sessions are short-lived, and the gateway refreshes cached entries every TTL
172   seconds or immediately on a DIDRevoked or ScoreUpdated event. Audit records are hashed into a batch
173   whose Merkle root is anchored on-chain asynchronously (step 7), so logging never delays the response.




174
175       Fig. 2. Per-request verification workflow with cached state reads and asynchronous audit anchoring (step 7).
176   A request is r = (DID, resource, operation, t, n, σ): a timestamp, a nonce and the device’s ECDSA
177   signature over the other fields. The PEP accepts it only if (i) |now − t| ≤ Δ and n is new within Δ, which
178   bounds replay; (ii) the DID is active in the cached registry, shown by a Merkle path against the latest



                                                               7
179   committed epoch root or by a direct chain read on a miss; (iii) σ verifies under the key whose hash is
180   registered; and (iv) the PDP finds the trust score and policy satisfied. Otherwise the request is denied and
181   an anomaly event is queued. The gateway also follows a fail-closed rule. If it cannot read the ledger, it
182   keeps using a cached entry past its TTL for at most k·TTL after the entry was last read (k = 2 by default,
183   parameter STALE_FACTOR) and then denies with reason “unavailable” until the ledger is back; k = 1
184   is the strict setting. A circuit breaker spares every request the read timeout while the source is down. The
185   first cloud experiments (Sections 6.1–6.9) used an earlier prototype that denied as soon as a failed read
186   followed TTL expiry (k = 1), and the k = 2 rule is evaluated in Section 6.10.

187   3.8. Ledger Access, Revocation and Read Quorum
188   A Merkle membership proof against an epoch root shows only that a DID was enrolled in that epoch. It
189   stays valid after revocation, so it says nothing about freshness. Revocation is therefore enforced through
190   the registry record (a revoked flag), which the gateway reads on a cache miss and which the
191   DIDRevoked event invalidates. A gateway also trusts the Besu node it reads from, and a compromised
192   node could return stale or forged state. A gateway without a validator of its own can instead read from
193   1 nodes pinned to the same block height and accept a state only if all answers agree: any f + 1 matching
194   answers include an honest node, and IBFT 2.0 has instant finality, so there are no forks to reconcile. The
195   prototype implements this option (QUORUM = 2 for n = 4); the default reads from one node, and the
196   cost of the quorum is reported in Section 6.10.

197   4. Use Case and Threat Model
198   4.1. Use Case
199   A smart factory has several production cells, each with PLCs and sensors behind one edge gateway; a
200   remote vendor engineer needs time-limited maintenance access to one PLC class; a SCADA historian
201   consumes sensor data. Cells, vendor and integrator are separate administrative domains that jointly
202   operate validators.

203   4.2. Assets and Trust Assumptions
204   Assets are device identities, policy and trust state, session tokens and audit records. We assume a
205   majority-honest validator set (at most f Byzantine), secure device key storage, and gateways that are
206   trusted for enforcement but may be compromised individually.

207   4.3. Adversary Model
208   A Dolev–Yao network adversary can eavesdrop, replay and inject messages; may compromise a subset
209   of devices (including their keys) and one gateway; and may include an insider with administrative
210   credentials in one domain but not in a validator quorum.

215   4.5. Security Goals
216   Authenticity of devices, least-privilege authorisation, revocation within a bounded delay, non-
217   repudiable tamper-evident audit, and availability of access decisions during ledger congestion.

218   4.6. Security Analysis
219   We argue each goal under the assumptions of Sections 4.2–4.3 and four more: ECDSA over secp256k1
220   is unforgeable under chosen-message attack, Keccak-256 is collision- and second-preimage-resistant,
221   gateway and device clocks differ by less than Δ/2, and the enrolment authority registers only genuine
222   devices. These are proof sketches, not machine-checked proofs; a ProVerif [40] model of the request
223   protocol remains future work.
224   S1 Authenticity. A request passes only if the signature recovers an address whose hash equals the key
225   hash registered for the claimed DID. A forger without the device key must break signature
226   unforgeability, and registering a new key needs the authority (updateKey, registerDID). S2 Replay. A
227   replayed request fails if it is older than Δ or its nonce was already seen by the same gateway within Δ.
228   The signed message carries no gateway identifier, so the same request can be replayed once at a different
229   gateway within Δ unless nonce caches are shared or the gateway identity is added to the signed fields;
230   we neither implemented nor tested this mitigation. S3 Revocation. Once the revocation transaction is
231   final (IBFT 2.0 finality is instant), a gateway that sees the event stops accepting the device within the
232   polling interval, and a gateway that misses it enforces the revocation when its cache entry expires, i.e.
233   within TTL. If consensus halts, the transaction cannot be finalised, so the bound runs from finalisation
234   and not from submission (Section 6.10). S4 Audit integrity. Every batch root is anchored on-chain, so a
235   stored record that is altered later no longer verifies, and changing an anchored root needs f + 1 validators.
236   Completeness is not guaranteed: a compromised gateway can drop records before they are anchored
237   (and up to the 1 s anchoring interval is exposed on a crash), and the prototype’s AuditLog accepts
238   anchors from any account. S5 Availability. Decisions never wait for consensus and continue from cache
239   for at most k·TTL when the ledger is unreachable (Section 3.7). A gateway whose node is compromised
240   can deny service or serve stale state unless the read quorum of Section 3.8 is used. Out of scope:
241   compromise of the enrolment authority, of f + 1 validators or of a device key before revocation, and
242   traffic analysis.

243   5. Implementation and Experimental Methodology
244   5.1. Besu Blockchain Network
245   We deployed a four-validator Hyperledger Besu v26.9.0 IBFT 2.0 network (f = 1, block period 2 s, chain
246   ID 2026, zero base fee, London EVM) in Docker on a GitHub-hosted 2-vCPU Linux runner for the
247   contract-level micro-benchmark. All validators share that host, so network delay is negligible and CPU
248   is contended. The end-to-end experiments ran separately on seven Google Cloud VMs (Section 5.6). A
249   fresh network was started for each of 3 micro-benchmark repetitions, with contracts compiled by
250   Solidity 0.8.20 (optimizer 200 runs, EVM “london”). Five scenarios used pre-signed transactions from
251   8 worker wallets: S1 sequential write confirmation, S2 read latency (500 eth_call evaluations of
252   AccessControl.evaluateRequest), S3 per-event logging at 10–200 tx/s offered for 20 s, S4 batch
253   anchoring at 1 tx/s, and S5 revocation visibility. Blocks were polled every 25 ms. Scripts, contracts and
254   raw results are in the public repository listed under Data Availability.

255   5.2. Smart Contracts
256   The four contracts total about 370 lines. A Hardhat/Chai suite of 15 tests passes and covers registration,
257   duplicate and unauthorised registration, revocation, key update, quorum scoring, score floor, rate
258   limiting, non-validator rejection, access grant/deny, session expiry, epoch Merkle membership and
259   audit batch inclusion proofs.

260   5.3. End-to-End Prototype and Load Generator
261   Besides the contract tests, we built the whole request path as running software (Node.js 20): a PEP/PDP
262   service, a load generator and the baselines of Section 5.4. Every device owns a secp256k1 key and signs
263   DID | resource | timestamp | nonce; its key hash and class are registered on-chain and its DID belongs to
264   one Merkle epoch commitment. The PEP rejects timestamps older than 5 s and repeated nonces,
265   recovers the signer, compares it with the registered key hash, checks the Merkle proof against the cached
266   epoch root and evaluates the ABAC policy (device class 0–1, trust score ≥ 50). In BC-ZTA, identity,
267   trust and policy state are read from Besu with eth_call on a cache miss and cached for 30 s. A block-
268   polling subscription (20 ms) invalidates an entry when a DIDRevoked, KeyUpdated or ScoreUpdated
269   event is mined, every decision is hashed, and once per second the Merkle root of the pending batch is
270   anchored with AuditLog.anchorBatch. The load generator emulates N = 10, 25, 50, 100, 200 devices
271   that send one request per second with staggered phases, after 5 s of warm-up and over a 20 s
272   measurement window; requests are signed beforehand so the generator is not the bottleneck. PEP,
273   directory server, load generator and the four validators run on separate machines (Section 5.6).

274   5.4. Baselines
275   (B1) Centralised PKI-IAM: one lookup per request at a directory server on its own VM, whose
276   responses are delayed by 40 ms with Linux tc netem to model a remote central service (about 50 ms per
277   lookup in total, Section 6.1). (B2) ABAC without blockchain: a local attribute store at the PEP. (B3)
278   Blockchain-only (sync): for every request the PEP reads state with eth_call, sends an AuditLog.logEvent
279   transaction (eight sender accounts) and replies only when the block containing it is observed. (B4)
280   Keycloak: a real Keycloak 26.4.0 issues each device an OpenID Connect access token whose claims
281   carry its class, trust score and key hash, and the PEP calls the token-introspection endpoint [41] on every
282   request; revocation disables the user. (B5) OpenLDAP: a real slapd directory holding the same
283   attributes, queried once per request [42]. B4 and B5 also run with a PEP-side decision cache (“cached”,
284   TTL 10 s), which is the usual way to cut their latency. (P) BC-ZTA as in Section 5.3. All systems use the

287   5.5. Performance Metrics
288   (E1) End-to-end latency (mean, P95) from request send to decision, goodput (accepted requests per

294   5.6. Distributed Deployment
295   The end-to-end experiments E1–E5 ran on Google Cloud (us-central1-a) on seven VMs in one VPC
296   without external addresses: four Besu validators (e2-medium, 2 vCPU, 4 GB, one validator each), a
297   PEP/PDP host (e2-standard-2), the directory server for B1 (e2-small) and a controller (e2-standard-2)
298   that runs the load generator and the experiment scripts and submits the on-chain transactions. Linux tc
299   netem delays all outgoing traffic of each validator VM by 5 ms, to stand for a plant-local validator
300   network, and the responses of the directory server by 40 ms, to stand for a remote central service; all
301   other delay is the real VM-to-VM delay. Each of the 5 repetitions started a freshly generated chain and
302   restarted the PEP for every device count, and the repetitions ran one after another on the same VMs. The
303   validator-failure test (E5) stops one validator container for 30 s while 50 devices are served. A single
304   cloud zone approximates independent hosts better than a shared CI runner, but it is not an industrial
305   network: devices are emulated and co-tenancy in the cloud adds variance. Deployment scripts,
306   configuration and raw results are in the public repository.
307   A second cloud run on the same VMs added Keycloak 26.4.0 (Docker, development mode, embedded
308   H2 database, OIDC token introspection) and OpenLDAP slapd (Debian 12 package, one search per
309   request) on the directory VM (B4, B5), the cached variants of both, a revocation test, a 25 s outage test,
310   an open-loop PEP capacity test and the flood sweep of Section 6.9. Besu ran with a 2.5 GB heap for the
311   flood sweep and all later runs; the first IAM repetition used the default 1 GB heap, which does not affect
312   the PEP-side numbers reported here.

336    The Besu micro-benchmark gave the following. Write confirmation takes 1522 ms on average at 10 tx/s
337    (P95 2439 ms), close to the 2 s block period; a transaction submitted just after a block, as in the phase-

342    Because all validators share one 2-vCPU host, 60 tx/s is a lower bound: on the seven-VM deployment

439   6.9. Ledger Flood Sweep
440   To test ledger flooding we swept the offered rate of audit-log transactions from 0 to 1,200 tx/s (16 spam
441   wallets, pre-signed transactions sent in nonce order, 20 s per rate after a 5 s warm-up, 50 devices at 1
442   request/s, 2 repetitions), once with BC-ZTA and once with the synchronous design, which writes every
443   decision as a transaction on the same ledger (Fig. 8). The committed rate saturates at about 193 tx/s for
444   BC-ZTA and 215 tx/s for the synchronous design (peak at 200–400 tx/s offered) and then declines, to
445   106 and 152 tx/s at 1,200 offered, because Besu rejects most of the excess (“nonce too distant” and, at
446   the highest rate, “transaction pool not enabled”; 50266 of 64802 submissions in the BC-ZTA run). The
447   access latency of BC-ZTA does not follow: its mean stays at 5.8–6.3 ms and its P95 at no more than 7.9
448   ms with every request answered, since decisions never wait for the ledger. The mean latency of the
449   synchronous design climbs from 1.6 s with an idle ledger to 24 s at 1,200 tx/s (P95 30 s), and 7% of its
450   requests fail at the highest rate. A flood thus denies service to ledger-bound designs but only delays BC-
451   ZTA’s own audit anchoring and revocation visibility, which we did not measure under flood.

523    6.11. Comparison with Baselines
524    BC-ZTA is 0.2 ms slower than a ledger-free ABAC store at 50 devices, but adds decentralised identity,
525    shared policy and tamper-evident anchored audit that B2 lacks. Against the centralised PKI-IAM it is
526    89% faster at 50 devices (with 40 ms of netem delay on the directory) and removes the central directory,
527    and against the synchronous blockchain it avoids about 1.4 s per decision and the ledger ceiling. Against
528    real Keycloak and OpenLDAP (Section 6.8) it is 8× and 7× faster at 100 devices, but a PEP-side cache
529    gives similar mean latency at the cost of stale revocations, and a central directory with pushed
530    revocations (Section 6.10) matches its latency and revokes faster. The price is revocation latency: about
531    1.3 s with events (one block) instead of about 84 ms for a central store. The Ethereum-based framework
532    mentioned in Section 1 pays about 25 ms extra per request even at small scale [17], in line with our
533    finding that consensus on the request path is the bottleneck.

534    6.12. Discussion and Limitations
535    The central trade-off is bounded revocation staleness in exchange for latency. The push baseline of
536    Section 6.10 shows that the trade-off is not specific to the ledger: a trusted central operator gets the same
537    latency and faster revocation, so the ledger has to be justified by who controls identity state and who can
538    verify the audit, not by speed. The main limitations are these. (i) The seven VMs sit in one zone of one
539    cloud provider, so inter-VM delay is that of a data-centre network plus the injected netem delay, not that
540    of a plant network or a WAN, and absolute latency and throughput are specific to the VM sizes. (ii) B1–


                                                                                    21
541   B3 are simple re-implementations; Keycloak ran in development mode (single node, embedded H2
542   database) and OpenLDAP as a single server, both behind an emulated 40 ms WAN, so tuned or clustered
543   deployments may behave differently, particularly for availability. (iii) Devices are emulated by a load
544   generator, with up to 200 devices at 1 request/s; no physical devices, industrial protocols, secure
545   elements, larger populations or bursty, churn-heavy workloads were used. (iv) One plant authority key
546   issues registrations and revocations, so authority compromise is out of scope. (v) The availability tests
547   are narrow: Besu rejected part of the E4 flood (50266 of 64802 submissions at 1,200 tx/s), E5 stops one
548   validator once for 30 s and Section 6.10 stops two for 40 s, and there is no network partition or Byzantine
549   validator. (vi) Experiments have five repetitions (three for the IAM tests, two for the flood sweep) on the
550   same VMs, the revocation tests use 8 devices per run, and a single-process PEP saturates at roughly
551   250–290 req/s, so larger sites need several PEPs (scale-out was measured on one host only). (vii) Trust
552   scores are voted values, not learned from data. Comparisons with published numbers are indicative
553   only, since workloads and testbeds differ.
554   The security analysis (Section 4.6) adds further limits. The nonce cache is per gateway and the signed
555   request carries no gateway identifier, so a captured request can be replayed once at another gateway
556   within Δ. The default read path trusts one ledger node (the quorum option is implemented, and its cost is
557   measured in Section 6.10, but it is not part of the default measurements). Audit completeness is not
558   protected against a compromised gateway, and the Merkle epoch proof shows enrolment, not freshness.
559   The device-side cost of signing and storing a proof was not measured, so compatibility with constrained
560   devices (D5) is an argument, not a result. Each request is signed individually, which suits access
561   decisions but not high-rate telemetry, where a session credential issued after one verified request would
562   be used instead. All statistical statements rest on 2–5 repetitions with standard deviations or 95%
563   confidence intervals and no hypothesis tests.
564   Several points matter for deployment. Validators must be run by independent parties (plant, integrator,
565   vendor) for the Byzantine assumption to mean anything. Device keys should sit in a secure element or
566   TPM, since the identity guarantees are only as strong as key custody. On-chain records hold key hashes,
567   scores and event hashes, not process data, which keeps operational data off the shared ledger. In the
568   prototype AuditLog and grantSession are callable by any account, and a production system would
569   restrict them to gateway identities. Finally, the fail-closed rule of Section 3.7 trades availability for
570   safety during ledger partitions and should be tuned against the plant’s uptime requirements.

571   7. Conclusion
572   We presented BC-ZTA, a blockchain-assisted zero-trust architecture that keeps consensus off the
573   request path by using edge-cached, Merkle-verified state and asynchronous batched anchoring. Four
574   Solidity contracts pass 15 unit tests, and measured gas shows that batch anchoring is 68–1351× cheaper
575   per record than per-event logging for batches of 50–1,000 records. On seven Google Cloud VMs with a
576   four-validator Besu network, BC-ZTA decided in 5.4 ms on average at 50 devices, served 200 devices
577   without failures with about one on-chain write per second, and kept deciding while one validator was
578   stopped. The synchronous design needed 1.4 s at 50 devices and answered only 40% of requests at 200
579   devices. The price is a revocation delay of about 1.3 s with event-driven invalidation. We also added a
580   central directory with pushed revocations to test whether the ledger is needed for speed. It has the same
581   latency and revokes in 109 ms, so the case for the ledger rests on removing any single operator’s control
582   over identity state and on audit that third parties can verify, not on performance. A fail-closed bound of



                                                          22
583   twice the cache lifetime, a read quorum of two validators, replicated gateways and the behaviour during
584   a consensus halt (revocations wait for consensus, here 108 s on average) are evaluated in Section 6.10.
585   All forged, replayed, stale, unregistered, tampered and policy-violating requests were rejected. The
